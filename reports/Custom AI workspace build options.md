# Build Thin, Route Cheap, Verify Everything

**Yes, build it, but build a thin version in stages and let a few cheap experiments decide how far to go.** At your usage (100 chat turns per day and 2 deep-research runs per day, which is 3,000 turns and 60 runs per 30-day month), a well-routed API workspace costs about **$0.15 to $0.32 per day ($4.40 to $9.70 per month)** if everything runs on the cheapest tier, about **$0.90 to $2.10 per day ($27 to $62 per month)** if cheap models read and a mid-tier model plans and writes, and **$5.70 to $10.75 per day ($171 to $323 per month)** if a frontier model does everything (my arithmetic on list prices; see the cost section for assumptions). A $20 subscription ($0.67 per day) cannot cover 60 research runs a month (ChatGPT Plus is reported at about 25), while $100 to $200 plans ($3.33 to $6.67 per day) probably can but lock you to one vendor, hide their limits, and cannot be used as the engine of your own app. Cheap models are **not** close to frontier models on hard, factual work (the best open model scores 46 against 65 for the best closed model on the main independent index), but they are close enough on routine work, and the cheapest closed models (Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6 Luna) are as good a "cheap tier" as the Chinese ones. The best base is **LibreChat (MIT license) behind a LiteLLM gateway, plus a separate research service you write yourself**, rather than a deep fork, Open WebUI (its license blocks white-labelling), or a from-scratch build. Selling this to small businesses works only as a platform fee, not as a markup on tokens, and Chinese first-party APIs should be an opt-in setting, not the default. The biggest unknown is whether a cheap-model research pipeline produces reports you trust: no published test exists for it, so run that experiment first.

## Every dollar figure follows the same usage and unit rules

All costs here use your stated usage: **100 chat turns per day (3,000 per month)** and **2 deep-research runs per day (60 per month)**, with a month defined as 30 days. Money for your usage is shown per day and per month together. Prices per token are always "per 1 million tokens" and are labelled that way. A token is a chunk of text, roughly three-quarters of a word; you pay for tokens going in (your prompt plus history) and tokens coming out (the answer). Cached tokens are input the provider already processed a moment ago and re-bills at a steep discount, 90% to 98% off depending on the provider ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing)).

The API cost tables use these assumptions (my arithmetic). Each chat turn is 8,000 input tokens (90% cached) and 600 output tokens. Each research run re-reads 500,000, 1,500,000 or 3,000,000 input tokens in total across all its steps (85% cached) and writes 30,000 output tokens. Search fees are excluded and added separately. These are scenarios, not measurements. Real data on tokens per run is scattered: LangChain measured about 0.58 to 2.07 million tokens per task for its open research agent on older models ([LangChain open_deep_research](https://github.com/langchain-ai/open_deep_research)), and a secondary table attributed to the DRACO benchmark lists 45,000 to 779,000 input tokens per task across commercial systems ([benchmarklist DRACO](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/draco/)), so 500,000 to 1,500,000 is the realistic range and 3,000,000 is a stress case.

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Gateway / router | A small server between your chat app and the model companies. It picks the model, counts tokens, and records cost |
| MCP | A standard plug that lets an AI app use outside tools (Gmail, GitHub, files). "MCP server" is one such plug |
| CVE | A public ID for a known security hole |
| Prompt injection | Hidden instructions inside a web page or email that trick the AI into doing something you did not ask |
| Evidence labels | **Independent** = measured by a neutral party (Artificial Analysis, Vals AI, Arena). **Vendor** = the model maker's own claim. **Estimate** = my arithmetic |

## Cheap models match frontier on routine work but trail on hard and factual work

The honest answer to "are cheap models close?" is "it depends on the test, and the tests that are hardest to game say no." On the most recent independent composite, Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5 (launched around 28 October 2026, with about 60% of questions private), the best open-weights model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, scores **46**, against **65** for Claude Fable 5.2, 62 for GPT-6.2 Astra and 55 for Claude Sonnet 5.5. The cheap open models people usually call "near frontier" score lower still: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 33 and GLM-5.3 32, both below Anthropic's own cheapest model, Claude Haiku 5.5, at 36 ([Artificial Analysis leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models), [AA methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking)). The gap looks much smaller on the older version of the index (3 points between best open and best closed before v5 against 12 at launch of v5), which is probably why the "open models are nearly there" story is common; part of the widening comes from the new harder private tests, not only from real change ([OfficeChai](https://officechai.com/ai/artificial-analysis-launches-intelligence-index-v5-with-majority-private-datasets-openai-models-gain-muse-wonder-kimi-drop/)). Scores from before and after v5 are not comparable.

The picture improves for cheap models on tasks closer to your daily work. On human-preference voting (Arena), the best open model, Kimi K3 at 1487, sits about 40 points below the leader at 1526, and above several current GPT entries; but this measures style on short chats, not reliability ([BenchLeader Arena mirror](https://www.benchleader.com/benchmarks/lmarena_text)). On Vals AI's enterprise index (finance, legal, tax, coding), DeepSeek V4.1 Flash scores **61.3%** against 71.3% for the leader, at **$0.66 per test against $17.94**, and on individual sub-tests it is within a few points of Sonnet 5.5 on legal research (45.2 against 48.1) and ahead on tax (44.4 against 42.0) ([Vals Index](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vals_index)). It is far behind on terminal-style agent work (31.8 against 64.1) and code migration (68.5 against 87.4), and weak on factual reliability.

Three findings change what "cheap" should mean. First, the cheap tier is not only Chinese. Per task, Haiku 5.5 costs $0.41 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash $0.60 on the AA index, while Haiku scores higher (36 against 33), and GPT-6.2 Sol scores 57 at $1.01 per task ([AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models)). On Vals, GPT-6.2 Sol (66.1% at $3.18 per test) beats both Kimi K3.1 Pro (62.5% at $6.44) and GLM-5.3 (59.9% at $8.52) on score and price ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vals_index)). So only the very cheap models, such as DeepSeek V4.1 Flash at $0.66 per Vals test, give a large saving, and the mid-priced Chinese models give little. Second, DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 is older, weaker (AA 23) and dearer than V4.1 Flash (AA 33) ([AA V4 Pro](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/deepseek-v4-pro)), so it should not be your "strong" Chinese model. Third, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is wordy: it produced 174 million output tokens on the index against a 101 million median ([AA DeepSeek Flash](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/deepseek-v4-1-flash-1017)), which erodes its low per-token price.

**Conflicts and weak spots.** Vendor tables look much better than independent ones because they use older benchmark versions and older rivals: DeepSeek's card reports 90.6 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 while Vals measures 31.8 on version 4.0 ([DeepSeek card](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash), [Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vals_index)). Kimi's own "hallucination bench" ranks Kimi above Opus 5.5 and every rival, but it is internal and unverified ([Kimi card](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3.1-Pro)). The NIST evaluation group (CAISI) put DeepSeek V4 Pro about eight months behind US models, relayed through a think-tank summary, and I did not read the primary report ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/what-know-about-chinese-ai-models)). Arena figures come from a mirror because the site itself blocked retrieval. My reading: use cheap models for retrieval, extraction, drafting from supplied sources, and chat; keep a stronger model for planning, final synthesis, and claim checking.

Chat alone is cheap on small models: 100 turns per day cost **$0.05 per day ($1.50 per month)** on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash off-peak, $0.047 per day ($1.42 per month) on Haiku 5.5, **$0.87 per day ($26.16 per month)** on Sonnet 5.5, and **$3.34 per day ($100.32 per month)** on Fable 5.2 (my arithmetic on the list prices cited above). Four caveats apply. DeepSeek charges double at peak hours (01:00 to 04:00 and 06:00 to 10:00 UTC on weekdays), so a daytime-heavy user lands nearer the peak figure. Gemini 4 Carbon doubles on 1 December 2026 and Gemini 3.8 Flash on 1 January 2027, so plan on the higher prices ([Google pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). Cache hits depend on timing: Anthropic and Kimi caches last 5 minutes by default, so a research agent re-reading its notes every few seconds will hit, but chat turns spaced over a day will often miss unless you pay for 1-hour caching on Anthropic (2 times the write price), while DeepSeek keeps caches for hours to days on a best-effort basis ([DeepSeek KV cache](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/guides/kv_cache), [Anthropic caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). Finally, DeepSeek raised its prices on 13 August 2026 (V4 Pro to $0.66 and $1.98 off-peak from $0.435 and $0.87), so the cheap end can move up ([InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/4209439/deepseek-raises-some-v4-prices-by-more-than-10x-as-ai-demand-strains-capacity.html)).

**Search fees come on top.** Anthropic and OpenAI charge $10 per 1,000 searches; at an assumed 40 searches per run that is 80 searches per day, or **$0.80 per day ($24 per month)** ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Google gives 5,000 grounded searches free per month, and DeepSeek stopped billing model-triggered search and fetch from 18 November 2026 ([DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing)). For a medium run (50 searches and 60 page fetches) the research notes list about $0.50 per run on Tavily ($1.00 per day, $30 per month), $0.35 on Exa ($0.70 per day, $21 per month), $0.25 on Brave ($0.50 per day, $15 per month) and $0.05 on Serper ($0.10 per day, $3 per month) ([Tavily](https://docs.tavily.com/documentation/api-credits), [Exa](https://exa.ai/docs/reference/pricing), [beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/tavily-vs-exa-vs-brave-vs-perplexity-search-api-agent-cost-2026)). On the cheapest models, search can cost as much as the model.

### Subscriptions and their limits

Consumer plans are priced around **$20 per month ($0.67 per day)** everywhere; the next steps are **$100 ($3.33 per day)** and **$200 ($6.67 per day)**, and ChatGPT Pro also has a $500 tier ($16.67 per day) ([ChatGPT pricing docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing), [Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing)). Business and Team seats are $20 per month annual ($0.67 per day) or $25 monthly ($0.83 per day) with a 2-seat minimum, so a solo user would pay for two seats, **$40 to $50 per month ($1.33 to $1.67 per day)** ([ChatGPT pricing docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing), [Anthropic Team update](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-team-updates)). The limits are the real issue, and none are published as hard numbers for deep research.

**The subsidy question has a split answer.** Sam Altman said in January 2025 that OpenAI was losing money on the $200 Pro plan because people used it more than expected ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/01/07/sam-altman-openai-chatgpt-pro-subscription-losing-money-tech/)). SemiAnalysis estimated in October 2026 that subscriptions are about 10% of Anthropic's revenue but can take over 40% of its inference compute, that a $200 OpenAI plan is exhausted after $2,897 of GPT-6 Astra usage at list price, and that a $200 Claude plan would have 50% left after $2,485 of Fable 5.1 usage ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/anthropic-subscriptions-offer-5x)). Those margins assume API list prices carry a 75% to 92% profit margin, so they say as much about API markups as about subscription losses, and a counter-argument holds that retail API prices sit well above serving cost ([GIGAZINE on SemiAnalysis](http://gigazine.net/news/20260612-anthropic-openai-subscription-margin/)). The evidence supports one conclusion: **heavy agentic use of the $100 to $500 tiers is plausibly subsidized; typical $20 chat use is not obviously subsidized**, and caps (5-hour windows, weekly limits, fallback to smaller models) are how vendors bound their losses. For you, the implication is practical. If you need frontier quality on every research run, your API-equivalent usage is **$10.75 per day ($322.60 per month)** on Fable 5.2, so a $100 or $200 plan is cheaper than the API if its limits fit, and that is the strongest argument for keeping a subscription. You also cannot use a subscription as the engine of your own app: Anthropic's Agent SDK terms bar third parties from offering claude.ai login or its rate limits in their products ([Agent SDK overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/overview)).

The routed estimate adds three numbers: chat on DeepSeek Flash at peak ($0.10 per day, $3.01 per month), research of $0.70 to $0.90 per day ($21 to $27 per month) from the cheap-reader design in the research-pipeline section, and Serper search ($0.10 per day, $3 per month), for the low end; the high end is chat on Flash plus all research on Kimi or Sonnet at the 1.5M scenario ($2.06 per day, $61.80 per month). **The honest read:** the build saves roughly $40 to $75 per month ($1.30 to $2.50 per day) against a $100 plan, or costs $7 to $42 more per month ($0.23 to $1.40 per day) than a $20 plan if your research fits inside that plan. ## LibreChat behind a gateway beats forking, Open WebUI, or starting from scratch

### Weighing the bases

| Build from components | You choose (MIT parts: assistant-ui, CopilotKit, LangGraph, Pydantic AI) | Full control of routing, context, cost | 6 to 9 weeks to a polished version, 3 to 4 for a rough one (my estimate; no published figure) | Fallback if LibreChat fights you |

Small projects fall out quickly: Open Canvas and LangChain's Agent Chat UI are archived, Chainlit's status conflicts between sources, Cherry Studio is AGPL desktop software, and Jan is a local desktop app ([Open Canvas](https://github.com/langchain-ai/open-canvas), [Agent Chat UI](https://github.com/langchain-ai/agent-chat-ui), [Cherry Studio](https://github.com/CherryHQ/cherry-studio), [Jan](https://github.com/janhq/jan)). If you build rather than fork, the live UI choices are Vercel AI SDK with the shadcn-hosted AI Elements (the old `ai-elements` package is deprecated at the end of November 2026), assistant-ui (MIT, branching and tool-call views) and CopilotKit (MIT) ([AI Elements](https://github.com/vercel/ai-elements), [assistant-ui](https://github.com/assistant-ui/assistant-ui), [CopilotKit](https://github.com/CopilotKit/CopilotKit)). For the agent loop, Anthropic's Claude Agent SDK is the wrong tool for a multi-vendor router, because Anthropic says it does not support routing Claude Code to non-Claude models through any gateway ([LLM gateway docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/llm-gateway)); Pydantic AI, LangGraph or the OpenAI Agents SDK talk to any OpenAI-compatible endpoint, though the last drops Responses-only features on other providers ([OpenAI Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/models/)).

**Recommended architecture.** Run LibreChat as the screen, point it at one model name served by a LiteLLM gateway, and put everything smart behind the gateway: the routing rules, the token and cost counting, budgets, retries and the translation of each provider's quirks. Build deep research as a separate service that LibreChat calls as a tool. This keeps the UI swappable: if LibreChat fights you, replace only the front end. The effort I estimate is 4 to 6 part-time weeks to a working version (days to deploy, about a week for routing and cost display, one to two weeks for research, a week for file generation, days for hardening); no source publishes such an estimate, so treat it as judgment.

### Per-task routing and cost tracking

The strongest evidence says to route by task, not by guessing difficulty. Independent testing (LLMRouterBench, January 2026, 21 datasets, 33 models, 10 routers) found many routers perform alike and several recent commercial ones fail to beat a plain best-single-model baseline ([arXiv 2601.07206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07206)). Savings claims come from the sellers: LiteLLM reports 40.4% cheaper at 97.1% relative quality on public benchmarks and 51.1% saved on its own production traffic, but against an all-flagship baseline, with an AI judge and a single run ([LiteLLM benchmarks](https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/auto_router/benchmarks)). Switching models mid-chat also forfeits the cache: "each model has its own cache," and a switch re-reads the whole history at full price, a loss of about 20 times on Sonnet 5.5 where a cache read costs 5% of input ([Claude Code caching docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching.md), [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). So the policy is: fixed rules by task type, a light classifier only for free-form chat, pin each conversation to its first model (LiteLLM's `session_affinity` does this), and allow a switch only when you click "think harder" or the tool loop fails.

**Showing cost always.** LibreChat already logs token transactions, shows a context gauge and has a `contextCost` option, but it prices from per-model rates in its config ([LibreChat token usage](https://librechat.ai/docs/configuration/token_usage)). If the gateway picks the model, LibreChat sees only "auto" and cannot price the reply. The fix is small but unavoidable: read the gateway's per-response cost header (`x-litellm-response-cost`) and display it, with the model actually used, a running day total, and the cache share ([LiteLLM cost tracking](https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/proxy/cost_tracking)). Bill from the provider's returned usage, never from local token counts: tokenizers differ, DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM and Kimi publish no official client-side counter, and Langfuse itself refuses to guess ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/docs/model-usage-and-cost)). Provider usage reports come in three shapes (OpenAI-style cached tokens included in the prompt count, Anthropic's three separate buckets, and Langfuse's exclusive buckets) and DeepSeek needs a peak and off-peak price table, so keep a versioned price table with effective dates. Use Langfuse (MIT apart from enterprise folders, owned by ClickHouse since January 2026) for traces and LiteLLM budgets for hard caps ([Langfuse repo](https://github.com/langfuse/langfuse)). Whether LibreChat displays per-message cost live for a routed model is unconfirmed; check it in the first spike.

### Security you must design in from day one

Both leading apps had serious holes in 2026, concentrated exactly where you want features: MCP, code execution, file handling and login tokens. LibreChat's list includes CVE-2026-22252 (critical, 9.1, command injection through MCP stdio servers), CVE-2026-32625 (secret leak through MCP URL placeholders), CVE-2026-34371 (file write through code execution) and CVE-2026-54030 (OAuth token theft, 9.3); Open WebUI's includes CVE-2026-56400 (9.6, a cross-origin flaw leading to remote code execution) and a cross-user code-interpreter exploit ([SentinelOne](https://www.sentinelone.com/vulnerability-database/cve-2026-22252/), [NVD](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-32625), [Wiz](https://www.wiz.io/vulnerability-database/cve/cve-2026-56400), [CraftedSignal](https://feed.craftedsignal.io/briefs/2026-07-open-webui-cross-user-rce/)). Trackers disagree on some scores and some Open WebUI CVEs were disputed, so confirm against the projects' own advisories ([Open WebUI disposition](https://docs.openwebui.com/security/vendor-dispositions/cve-2026-0767/)). Guardrails that follow from the pattern: keep it off the public internet (VPN or an access proxy), allow stdio MCP servers only through admin config, run code and servers in containers with no network, pin versions and read advisories before each upgrade, and note that LibreChat's latest releases are tagged pre-release (the last untagged one is v0.8.7 from June).

### Three licensing facts

LibreChat's MIT license permits rebranding and resale, but ClickHouse bought it on 4 November 2025 and staff promised the license stays; commenters called the promise non-binding, and MIT means the community can always fork ([ClickHouse](https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-acquires-librechat), [Hacker News](https://hn.jakechampion.name/item/45877770)). Open WebUI forbids removing its branding beyond 50 users without a paid enterprise license, price unpublished ([Open WebUI license](https://docs.openwebui.com/license/)). Writing your own app avoids all of this, at the cost of weeks.

### The pipeline I recommend

Follow the orchestrator-and-workers pattern Anthropic describes: one lead agent plans, several workers search in parallel, a separate citation step attaches sources; Anthropic reports that agents use about 4 times and multi-agent systems about 15 times the tokens of chat, and that token use alone explained 80% of the variance in its browsing evaluation ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Concretely: a strong model clarifies and plans (one call); a handful of cheap workers each search, rank results, fetch pages and compress them to notes; the planner does at most one or two gap-check rounds; a strong model writes the report; and a cheap pass checks every cited claim against the stored page text. Save every fetched page by address so repeat runs are free, keep the prompt start byte-stable so the cache hits, and cap each run with an "answer with what you have" stop. A reference build from Ubicloud with 24B to 32B open models reached under $0.10 per run but warned that the naive search-and-write version "looked polished but hallucinated" ([Ubicloud](https://www.ubicloud.com/blog/rebuilding-deep-research-with-open-models)). For search, a cheap mix works: DeepSeek's search and fetch endpoints ($0.75 per 1,000 requests each, not billed when the model triggers them from 18 November 2026) or Serper (about $1 per 1,000) for discovery, a plain page fetch first, then Jina Reader or Firecrawl ($1 per 1,000 pages on the Standard plan) for pages that need script rendering or PDFs, and SearXNG as a free but unreliable fallback ([DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing), [Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/pricing)). Page text, not the search call, is the main token cost: one model puts it at 6 to 20 times the search fee ([beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/tavily-vs-exa-vs-brave-vs-perplexity-search-api-agent-cost-2026)), and GPT Researcher reports that skipping its context filter raises cost 65% per report ([GPT Researcher](https://github.com/assafelovic/gpt-researcher)).

Expose it as a job, not one long call: `start_research(question, effort)` returns an ID after an optional plan approval, a status call reports phase, searches run, pages read, tokens and dollars spent live, and the finished report lands as a Markdown file with sources. This mirrors Gemini's background-and-poll design ([Google docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/agents/use-deep-research)). Offer three effort levels with hard dollar caps that I suggest (about $0.50 quick, $1.50 standard, $4 deep per run, my numbers) and an "estimate before you run" range built from the 4 times and 15 times priors. Non-urgent runs can wait for DeepSeek's off-peak half price, saving roughly $2 to $4 per month ($0.07 to $0.13 per day) on Flash and $20 to $60 per month ($0.67 to $2 per day) on Pro in my scenarios.

### What the quality evidence supports

Quality data is the weak link. On DeepResearch Bench, LangChain's open agent running GPT-5 scored about 50 against 48.9 for Gemini Deep Research under the original judge, so an open pipeline on a strong model can reach commercial range ([DeepResearch Bench paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11763), [open_deep_research](https://github.com/langchain-ai/open_deep_research)). But the leaderboard changed its judge in May 2026, scores before and after are not comparable, and I found **no entry for DeepSeek, GLM or Kimi** inside any open pipeline ([benchmark README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Ayanami0730/deep_research_bench/main/README.md)). The second version of the benchmark found even the best systems met under half the rubric items ([arXiv 2601.08536](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08536v1)). The model-capability data adds the central risk: open models hallucinate more, so a verification step and a strong final model are not optional. The plan is to test your pipeline against your own questions before trusting it.

## Agents, connectors, files and context are mostly assembly work

### Pre-built agents, connectors and MCP

The only portable agent format that exists is **Agent Skills**: a folder with a `SKILL.md` file, optional scripts and references, loaded only when needed ([agentskills.io](https://agentskills.io/home)). Anthropic's skills repository (197k stars; document skills stated as Apache-2.0 in its README, with a note to check each skill's license) is the best starting library ([anthropics/skills](https://github.com/anthropics/skills)). Treat a "pre-built agent" as a skill, a tool allow-list and a routing preset in a small manifest, and ship a few in your repository: Research, Inbox triage (draft-only), Meeting prep, Document builder, Repo reviewer. Skills are code-adjacent, so review them like dependencies.

MCP is now a stateless, remote-first protocol (spec revision 2026-07-28 removed sessions and deprecated dynamic client registration in favour of client ID metadata documents), so build against remote Streamable HTTP with OAuth and treat local stdio servers as a sandboxed convenience ([MCP changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog)). Directory counts are marketing: estimates range from about 21,000 to over 100,000 servers, and Anthropic archived its reference servers for GitHub, Slack, Google Drive, Postgres and others, calling the remainder demo-grade ([modelcontextprotocol/servers](https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/servers), [PulseMCP](https://www.pulsemcp.com/servers)). Prefer official vendor servers: Google now offers official remote Workspace servers (Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Calendar) in developer preview, with an MIT self-hosted alternative at 3.5k stars ([Google guide](https://developers.google.com/workspace/guides/configure-mcp-servers), [google_workspace_mcp](https://github.com/taylorwilsdon/google_workspace_mcp)). For many apps at once, free tiers are ample for one user (Composio 10,000 calls per month, Arcade 2,000), while self-hosted Activepieces (MIT) avoids per-user fees; Nango is source-available and Pipedream charges $2 per external user beyond 100 ([Composio](https://composio.dev/pricing), [Arcade](https://www.arcade.dev/pricing), [Activepieces](https://github.com/activepieces/activepieces), [Pipedream](https://pipedream.com/pricing)).

| Connector decision | Pick for you | Why | Later, for SMBs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Workspace | Official Google remote MCP or the MIT server, your own OAuth client, read plus drafts | Personal use avoids Google's paid security assessment | Storing customer mail on servers can trigger it ([Google](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/production-readiness/restricted-scope-verification)) |
| GitHub | The official GitHub server | Reference server is archived | Per-session repo scoping |
| Search and fetch | A search API plus a fetch tool | Cheapest research input | Shared quotas |
| Many other apps | Composio free tier or Activepieces | No per-call cost | Nango or Pipedream Connect |

Two cost traps. First, tool definitions eat the context: a typical five-server setup is about 55,000 tokens, and accuracy degrades beyond roughly 30 to 50 tools; Anthropic's code-execution approach cut one example from 150,000 to 2,000 tokens, a 98.7% reduction ([tool search docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/tool-search-tool), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/code-execution-with-mcp)). Anthropic's tool search only works with Claude models, so build a small model-agnostic "search tools" function and keep fewer than about 20 tools loaded per agent. Second, security. The documented danger is the combination of untrusted content, private data and a way to send things out: researchers made an agent using the official GitHub MCP server leak private repositories after reading a malicious public issue ([Invariant Labs](https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-github-vulnerability)); a look-alike `postmark-mcp` package silently copied every email to its author ([ATLAS case study](https://www.startupdefense.io/mitre-atlas-case-studies/aml-cs0053-poisoned-postmark-mcp-server-email-exfiltration)); and OWASP now keeps an MCP Top 10 ([OWASP](https://owasp.org/www-project-mcp-top-10/)). The minimum rules: an allow-list of pinned servers, re-approval when a tool's description changes, read tools automatic but write, send and delete tools approved with the exact arguments shown (no "always allow" for sending), least-privilege scopes, containers for local servers, and an audit log.

### File generation: let the model write a spec, let code build the file

Claude and ChatGPT-style products make Word, Excel, PowerPoint and PDF files by having the model write code in a sandbox, then rendering the result to check it ([Anthropic support](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/12111783-create-and-edit-files-with-claude)). Anthropic's open skills show the method: pptxgenjs for new decks, openpyxl for spreadsheets with a LibreOffice recalculation step (formulas have no cached values until recalculated), then LibreOffice to PDF and a look at the page images to catch overflow ([pptx skill](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/pptx/SKILL.md), [xlsx skill](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/xlsx/SKILL.md)). The best evidence on failures is a 2026 office-work benchmark: single-turn models were weak, code-execution errors caused most zero scores, especially for PowerPoint and Excel, and agents with a code tool improved PowerPoint scores ([OfficeEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10956)). My inference is that the way to make cheap models reliable is to shrink their job: the model emits a structured spec (slides, sheets, sections as JSON or Markdown), and your own templates built on pptxgenjs, openpyxl, docx-js or HTML-to-PDF render it, which removes most code errors. No controlled study compares cheap and frontier models on this, so test it (experiment 5). Parse uploaded files once with Docling (MIT) or PaddleOCR-VL (Apache-2.0), cache the Markdown, and feed the model text rather than raw PDFs each turn ([Docling](https://github.com/docling-project/docling)). Sandbox cost is negligible: a managed 2 vCPU, 4 GB sandbox runs about $0.17 per hour (roughly $0.003 per minute), so compute is fractions of a cent per file ([Fly.io](https://fly.io/learn/ai-sandbox-pricing/)); self-host with Docker plus gVisor or E2B's Apache-2.0 runtime, and avoid Daytona, whose open repository was archived on 3 October 2026 ([E2B](https://github.com/e2b-dev/E2B), [Daytona](https://github.com/daytonaio/daytona)).

### Context, caching and memory

The rule that saves the most money is to keep the start of every prompt identical from turn to turn. Providers cache by prefix: DeepSeek, Kimi, Z.ai and Qwen (implicit mode) cache automatically with no write fee, Anthropic and OpenAI add optional write charges, and every one of them loses the cache if you edit anything earlier in the prompt, change the tool list, put a timestamp at the top, or switch models ([Anthropic caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching), [OpenAI caching](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching), [Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). On DeepSeek Flash a cache hit costs $0.003 against $0.15 for a miss per 1M tokens off-peak, a 50-fold swing ([DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing)). Because of that, compaction is not free savings: a summary rewrites the start of the prompt and forces a full-price miss, which can cost more than it saves on cheap, cached models (my inference; no source measured it).

| Context technique | Evidence | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Stable prefix, append-only history | OpenAI, Anthropic and Manus agree | Always |
| Mask old tool results with a stub that keeps the URL or file path | JetBrains and TUM: about half the cost with equal or better solve rate than LLM summaries, on one coding benchmark ([arXiv 2508.21433](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.21433)) | First step |
| Summary compaction | Works but costlier and breaks caches | Rare, at 60% to 80% of the window |
| Retrieval of chunks added at the end of the prompt | Retrieval is 5 to 26 times cheaper per query than stuffing the window in small studies, though stuffing was more accurate in one ([arXiv 2606.20898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20898)) | Large file sets |
| Keep context small even with 1M windows | Accuracy fell as input grew in 18 models ([Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)) | Always |

For projects, pin the instructions and small files in the cached prefix and retrieve larger files through a tool (BM25 plus embeddings; Qwen3-Embedding and BGE-M3 are the common open choices). For memory, use LibreChat's built-in form: short key-value facts with a token cap, edited by the user and injected late so edits do not break the cache ([LibreChat memory](https://www.librechat.ai/docs/features/memory)). Skip graph memory (Graphiti needs a graph database and a strong structured-output model) and do not choose a memory product from its benchmarks: Mem0, Zep and Letta each report wins on LoCoMo and dispute each other's methods ([Letta](https://www.letta.com/blog/benchmarking-ai-agent-memory), [Mem0](https://github.com/mem0ai/mem0)).

### The browser agent: schedule it last and keep a human on every send

The open options are browser-use (MIT, 121k stars), Stagehand (MIT), Playwright MCP (Apache-2.0, with an extension mode that reuses your logged-in Chrome), Nanobrowser (Apache-2.0) and Skyvern (AGPL) ([browser-use](https://github.com/browser-use/browser-use), [Playwright MCP](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright-mcp), [Nanobrowser](https://github.com/nanobrowser/nanobrowser), [Skyvern](https://github.com/Skyvern-AI/skyvern)). Reliability claims shrink under independent testing: a human-checked study of live sites found most frontier agents complete only about 30% of tasks, and browser-use's own 89.1% on WebVoyager used a modified harness while an independent run scored 60.2% ([Online-Mind2Web](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01382), [Browser Use report](https://browser-use.com/posts/sota-technical-report)). Prompt injection is the central risk. Anthropic's own pilot measured attack success of 23.6% without defences and 11.2% with them in 2025, later reaching 2.0% on one model with improved safeguards, and Brave showed a hidden Reddit comment steering Perplexity's Comet browser into reading a user's email and leaking a one-time code ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-chrome), [Anthropic defences](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses), [Brave](https://brave.com/blog/comet-prompt-injection/)). Playwright MCP itself warns it is "not a security boundary."

| Site | Official route | Browser automation risk |
|---|---|---|
| Gmail | OAuth API; Google's Gmail MCP (developer preview) offers read and draft scopes; whether it can send is disputed between sources ([Google Workspace blog](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/05/agent-tools-and-security-updates-for-workspace-developers.html)) | Low if you use the API and draft only |
| LinkedIn | No general consumer API | User Agreement section 8.2 bans bots and automated methods including browser add-ons; accounts can be restricted ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement)) |
| Instagram | Graph API for professional accounts only ([Instagram Platform](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/instagram-platform/overview)) | Meta requires written permission for automated data collection ([Meta](https://www.facebook.com/legal/automated_data_collection_terms)); I could not read Instagram's own terms text |

My design rules: use official APIs where they exist (Gmail drafts, Instagram for your own business account), treat LinkedIn as a copilot that prepares text while you click, run the agent in a separate browser profile with only the accounts it needs, enforce per-site allow-lists outside the model, never combine email contents and open browsing in one context, and require approval for every irreversible step. Pick a vision-capable model that is actively maintained; ByteDance's UI-TARS repository was archived on 9 November 2026.

## Selling to small businesses works only as a platform fee, and Chinese APIs need a customer switch

### Market and pricing

The $20 to $30 per seat price point is crowded and getting cheaper with no real minimums: ChatGPT Business and Claude Team are $20 per user per month annual ($0.67 per day), Microsoft 365 Copilot Business $21, Gemini is bundled into Workspace from about $14, Onyx is $20, Langdock €23.20, and Abacus ChatLLM $10 ([ChatGPT docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing), [Claude Team](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-team-updates), [Office Watch](https://office-watch.com/2025/microsoft-365-copilot-business-ai-add-on/), [Onyx](https://onyx.app/pricing), [Langdock](https://www.langdock.com/pricing), [Abacus](https://chatllm.abacus.ai/)). The bring-your-own-key niche already has TypingMind ($99 per month for 5 users, API billed separately by the customer's own keys), AnythingLLM, Msty and OpenRouter ([TypingMind](https://docs.typingmind.com/typingmind-team/getting-started/typingmind-team-vs-claude-team)). I found **no evidence of traction or revenue for any bring-your-own-key vendor** and no survey showing small businesses pay extra for token transparency. The best demand signal is different: privacy and security is the top reported barrier to small-business AI use (33% in one US survey, 49% in another), and 33% of small-business owners spend nothing on AI tools ([Bluevine](https://www.bluevine.com/blog/small-business-ai-trends-report-2026), [Upwork](https://www.upwork.com/resources/state-of-ai-in-smbs)). The Cursor billing backlash of 2025 shows surprise usage bills are punished, which supports spend caps and visible cost as features ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/cursor-apologizes-unclear-pricing-changes-225709399.html)).

| Pricing model | Example | Fits you? |
|---|---|---|
| Markup on tokens | OpenRouter takes 5.5% to 8% on credits; BYOK is free up to $25,000 per month, then 5% ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/pricing)) | No: 5% of a $60 monthly bill is $3 per month ($0.10 per day) per customer |
| Flat workspace fee, customer pays provider | TypingMind $99 per month for 5 users | Yes, closest analogue |
| Per seat | $10 to $30 per user | Yes, but you meet ChatGPT Business on price |
| Seat plus usage credits | Dust, Langdock, Abacus | Needs you to hold keys and money |

**Is any fee justified?** Yes, but only for things a bring-your-own-key customer cannot get alone: hosting and updates, shared prompts and agents, admin spend caps, per-user usage analytics, governed routing, an audit trail and support. A flat $99 to $200 per workspace per month is the defensible range. My arithmetic on break-even for $5,000 net per month: about 55 to 60 customers at $99 flat, about 45 at $12 per seat and 10 seats, about 25 to 28 at $20 per seat and 10 seats, assuming 1 to 2 hours of support per customer per month at a notional $50 per hour (unsourced); hosting is only about €2 to €15 per tenant per month on small European VMs, so support time dominates ([Hetzner](https://www.hetzner.com/cloud/)). Customer acquisition and churn are unsourced and would lengthen the timeline. Key management is also a hidden cost: asking non-technical owners to create and top up API keys is friction that bundled seats avoid.

### Chinese-API data concerns

Using Chinese APIs is legal for private firms in the US and EU, but the risk is real. DeepSeek's privacy policy states data is stored in the People's Republic of China, lists model training as a purpose with an opt-out, and sets no fixed retention period; I found no separate API terms ([DeepSeek privacy policy](https://cdn.deepseek.com/policies/en-US/deepseek-privacy-policy.html)). A US Department of Homeland Security order dated 24 July 2026 bars DeepSeek products from civilian federal procurement, and law firms read it as flowing down to contractors; at least 17 states ban it on government devices ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13336.html), [Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-DeepSeek-Ban-Creates-New-Compliance-and-Reporting-Obligations-for-Contractors), [BGR](https://bgr.com/2014815/deepseek-illegal-us-states)). European regulators acted against the consumer app (Italy in January 2025; Berlin in June 2025 said the China transfer breaches GDPR), though those are regulator positions, not court rulings ([Heise](https://heise.de/-10462419)). NIST's CAISI found DeepSeek models more susceptible to hijacking ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-deepseek-security-gaps-caisi-study/)). Your assumption that small businesses do not mind is untested: I found no survey, and law, health, finance, federal contractors and anyone handling EU personal data are the likely objectors, while marketing, e-commerce and agencies probably are not.

The mitigation is cheap. The same open weights run at Western hosts at about first-party list price for GLM-5.3 ($1.40 and $4.40) and Kimi K3.1 Pro ($2 and $10 per 1M tokens), with a US geo-pinned option at 25% more on Together; DeepSeek V4 Pro costs about twice DeepSeek's off-peak rate on those hosts ([OpenRouter GLM-5.3](https://openrouter.ai/z-ai/glm-5.3/providers), [OpenRouter Kimi](https://openrouter.ai/moonshotai/kimi-k3.1-pro/providers), [Together](https://www.together.ai/pricing)). Two limits: OpenRouter does not state provider locations, and EU-routed options were thin (only Mistral and Inceptron for GLM-5.3, none for DeepSeek V4 Pro or Kimi K3.1 Pro). Build routing as a per-workspace policy with three tiers: Western closed models (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google); Western-hosted open weights; and first-party Chinese APIs only by explicit admin opt-in. For your own solo use, public-web research is a reasonable place to use DeepSeek's own cheap API, since the content is public; keep client files and personal mail on the first two tiers. Making routing a customer choice also keeps you from inheriting liability for a default that sends customer data to China.

Other business facts: if you build your own app, no upstream license limits you; the Claude Agent SDK bars offering claude.ai login in a product ([Agent SDK overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/overview)); a vendor-hosted service brings GDPR processor duties, security questionnaires and, if you store customers' Gmail data, Google's yearly security assessment (third parties quote $3,000 to $75,000, unofficial). Customer-hosted deployment lowers your liability but raises support cost and weakens recurring revenue.

## Verdict, phased plan, biggest unknowns and the experiments to run first

### The verdict

Build a thin version for yourself first. The value is control and visibility, not the monthly saving, which is only $40 to $75 against a $100 plan and negative against a $20 plan if research fits. Do not clone Claude: take LibreChat as the screen, LiteLLM as the brain, a small research service as the main feature, Anthropic's open skills for files, and a short list of vetted connectors. Commercialization is a separate bet: the market is crowded, bring-your-own-key demand is unproven, and key friction is real; decide after the personal version works and five customer interviews. If the Phase 2 quality gate fails, keep a subscription for research and keep the workspace for chat, cost visibility and files.

| Phase | Timing (part-time, my estimate) | What gets built | Gate to continue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Prove it | Week 1 | The experiments below; keep your subscription | Cheap-tier chat judged acceptable; research test not clearly worse |
| 1. Chat workspace | Weeks 2 to 3 | LibreChat plus LiteLLM, rule-based routing, fixed model pinning per chat, cost display, Langfuse, behind a VPN | Chat alone under $1 per day ($30 per month) |
| 2. Deep research | Weeks 4 to 6 | Research service with job status, search stack, verification pass, effort caps, page cache | Blind score within reach of your subscription on 10 questions; cost per run at or under $0.50 |
| 3. Files, projects, memory, agents | Weeks 7 to 9 | Spec-to-file renderers with LibreOffice checks, project instructions, capped memory, 3 to 5 skill agents, Google and GitHub connectors | Files pass a validator at least 9 times in 10 |
| 4. Browser agent | Later | Separate service, draft-only Gmail first, human approval on every send, no LinkedIn automation | Injection tests pass |
| 5. SMB pilot | Optional | 3 to 5 design partners, per-workspace provider policy, hosting | Paid commitments before more building |

Gate thresholds are my suggestions, not sourced.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. The cost decision is not "Chinese models against American ones" but "which tier does each step deserve," and the surprise is how little the mid-priced Chinese models add: on independent per-task cost, GPT-6.2 Sol beats Kimi K3.1 Pro and GLM-5.3, and only the very cheap DeepSeek Flash class opens a large price gap. The money that matters sits in research runs and in cache hits, so the workspace's most valuable parts are the unglamorous ones: a gateway that pins each conversation, a price table that counts every cached token, and a verification step that offsets the hallucination gap.

It also shows why the build is a reasonable bet but not a clear financial win. Against a $20 plan it saves nothing, and against a $100 plan it saves about $1.30 to $2.50 per day ($40 to $75 per month); what you gain is control, visibility and a base you could sell. That base is only worth selling if small businesses care about transparency and data control, and no evidence yet shows they pay for it. Run the first experiment this week, because one blind comparison of cheap-model research against your subscription settles the most expensive uncertainty in the whole plan.
